A risk control that can switch itself off forever

Drawdown controllers shrink a position as losses accumulate, reaching zero exposure at a chosen limit. But zero exposure means zero returns, which means wealth stops moving, which means the drawdown never recovers. The controller has no way back. Below is a real momentum strategy on the S&P 500 from 2004 to 2026, run through that controller. Move the exponent down and watch what the 2020 crash does.

The controller multiplies position size by ((C − D) / C)κ, where D is the drawdown from the running peak and C is the limit. At κ = 1 this is the cushion-proportional rule that Grossman and Zhou derived in 1993 as optimal under geometric Brownian motion. Lower exponents derisk earlier; higher ones hold size longer and cut harder near the limit.

The failure is structural rather than a matter of tuning. Once the multiplier reaches exactly zero the portfolio is frozen, so drawdown is frozen, so the multiplier stays at zero regardless of what the market does afterwards. A re-entry rule of the form “resume once drawdown falls below half the limit” cannot help, because the quantity it waits on can no longer change. The fix is a floor: never let exposure reach zero.

Everything above runs in your browser on real daily returns. The strategy is deliberately ordinary — twelve-month momentum, long or flat — so that what you see is the behaviour of the risk layer and not of a clever signal.

Power-law drawdown-modulated position sizing

Working paper · posted to SSRN

Treats the sizing exponent as the object of study rather than a fixed choice. Finds that the growth-optimal exponent is usually just the smallest one that avoids terminating the strategy, that the survival boundary depends only on volatility relative to the limit and not on the limit itself, and that under heavy-tailed returns no exponent satisfies the constraint at all.

Trade classification error in tick-level futures data

Working paper · posted to SSRN

Standard algorithms infer whether a trade was buyer or seller initiated, and are usually judged on average accuracy. Testing against exchange aggressor flags on 14.3 million CME E-mini S&P 500 futures trades finds the opposite of the expected failure mode: quote-based methods are essentially exact, the classical five-second quote lag is a pure timestamp artifact that makes things worse, and the tick rule fails most in calm markets, not volatile ones — with a measurable, mechanistic effect on order flow imbalance downstream.
